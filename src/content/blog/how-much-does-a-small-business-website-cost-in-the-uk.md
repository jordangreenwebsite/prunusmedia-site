A small business website can cost a few hundred pounds or several thousand. Neither price tells you much on its own. A simple three-page website for a new sole trader is a different job from a custom twenty-page website with service pages, original copy, quote forms and search research.

The useful question is not just what the website costs to build, but what is included, what you will pay to keep it running and whether it will help customers choose your business.

## UK website costs in 2026: a useful starting point

These are broad planning ranges, not official averages or fixed quotes. Published UK pricing guides vary in what they include, so compare the scope before comparing prices.

| Website approach | Illustrative initial cost | Suitable for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Build it yourself | £0 to £500, plus your time and subscriptions | A straightforward site you are happy to manage |
| Template-based freelancer build | £500 to £2,500 | A modest brochure site with limited custom work |
| Custom small-business website | £1,500 to £8,000 | Tailored design, content, services and enquiry journeys |
| Ecommerce or bespoke functionality | Several thousand pounds upwards | Selling online, complex bookings or business integrations |

For context, compare the published estimates from [Yorkshire Digital](https://www.yorkshiredigital.co.uk/articles/small-business-website-cost-uk) and [2xdev](https://www.2xdev.com/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost-uk). Their ranges are examples of industry guides, not a nationally representative survey. Ask whether VAT, content, hosting and maintenance are included.

## Why do two five-page websites cost different amounts?

A cheap build might use a ready-made template and words you supply. A more involved project may include interviews, competitor research, service-page planning, custom layouts, photography, copywriting, accessible forms, mobile testing and launch support.

Both may have five pages, but the work is very different.

A website that needs to attract enquiries also needs a sensible structure. Someone searching for a specific service should be able to find a page that explains that service properly. A single generic services page is not always enough. Equally, twenty near-identical location pages are not automatically better than five genuinely useful ones.

## Understand the difference between building, hosting and SEO

**Website build:** the design, development, content and testing required to create the site.

**Hosting:** the infrastructure that keeps the site available online. Some providers bundle domain management, security and support with this.

**Website care:** updates, monitoring, repairs and agreed content changes. Ask what is actually covered.

**SEO:** research and improvements intended to help the website appear for relevant searches. Technical SEO foundations should be part of a good build, but ongoing optimisation, content and reporting are separate work.

Do not assume that paying monthly means ongoing SEO is included. Likewise, a large upfront build fee does not necessarily include long-term support.

## Compare the total cost, not just the first payment

A website quoted at £995 upfront plus £99 per month costs **£2,183 over the first twelve months**, before separately charged extras. That is simple arithmetic, not a recommendation of a particular package.

Here are two fictional offers to show why the contract matters:

| Example | Upfront | Monthly | First year | Three years |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A: lower upfront fee with care | £600 | £65 | £1,380 | £2,940 |
| B: higher upfront fee with basic hosting | £1,800 | £25 | £2,100 | £2,700 |

These examples assume unchanged monthly charges and exclude VAT, domain renewals and extra work. Offer A costs less initially but more over three years. Whether that matters depends on the support and services included.

Before signing, ask how long the contract lasts, whether prices change at renewal, what happens if you cancel and whether you can move your website elsewhere.

## Hidden costs to check

### Domain and business email

Confirm who controls the domain registration, how renewals work and whether email hosting is included. Ideally your business should retain control of its domain account even if an agency manages the website.

### Words and images

Who writes the pages? Who provides the photographs? A good-looking template without clear explanations of your services is not a finished sales tool. If stock images are used, they should never be presented as completed customer projects.

### Contact forms, bookings and payments

A form is only useful if enquiries arrive reliably. Ask for a real test of form delivery, mobile navigation and click-to-call links. Booking tools and payment providers may carry additional subscription or transaction charges.

### Updates and technical support

Find out who fixes a broken form, updates your opening hours, manages redirects and helps when something stops working. Basic hosting and managed website care are not the same service.

### Search visibility

A technically sound launch should include clear titles and headings, mobile usability, appropriate internal links, canonical URLs and a sitemap. None of these guarantees first place on Google. Search performance also depends on competition, relevance, useful content and the business's wider reputation.

## How many pages should a small business pay for?

There is no magic number. Start with the information a customer needs to decide whether to contact you: what you do, who you help, where you work, proof of your work and how to get a quote.

A roofer may need distinct pages for roof repairs, pitched roofing, flat roofing and roofline work. An accountant may need separate pages for bookkeeping, payroll and tax services. These pages earn their place when they answer different questions, not because a designer wants to increase the page count.

If the business only needs a simple online presence, a smaller site may be the better investment. Build the right structure for the business today and leave room to expand when there is a genuine reason.

## Questions to ask before accepting a quote

1. What pages, writing, images and features are included?
2. What is the complete first-year cost, including VAT where applicable?
3. What will the website cost over the full contract?
4. Who owns and controls the domain, files and content?
5. What do monthly payments cover, and what costs extra?
6. How are forms, mobile navigation, redirects and SEO basics tested?
7. What happens if you want to change provider or add more services?

A written scope protects both sides. It makes it much easier to tell whether two quotes are genuinely comparable.

## What is the right budget for your business?

The right budget is the smallest amount that buys a website capable of doing the job properly. A new business testing an idea may not need a large bespoke build. An established business with several valuable services, competitive local searches and a complicated enquiry process may benefit from deeper planning and custom work.
